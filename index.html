The verdict is computed under one set of assumptions. Real outcomes depend on things this model can't see. Skim before acting on any number above:

  • Emergency reserves. If the down payment + closing leaves you with less than 6 months of expenses, the math is lying — most foreclosures begin with a thin reserve, not a bad mortgage.
  • Retirement contribution displacement. If buying causes you to drop 401(k) from 15% to 6% to "afford it," the long-run hit often dwarfs the entire buy-vs-invest gap.
  • Concentration / leverage risk. A 20%-down purchase is 5× leverage on a single, illiquid, undiversified asset correlated to your local job market.
  • Income volatility. Renter's downside on a layoff is a moving truck. Owner's is a forced sale in a soft market plus credit destruction. The model assumes income holds.
  • Insurance availability + repricing. CA / FL / LA insurance has run up 30–100% in 3 years; some homes are now uninsurable. Held flat in the model.
  • HOA special assessments. 5-figure surprises are routine in condos post-Surfside. Not modeled.
  • Single-home idiosyncratic risk. Foundation failure, neighborhood decline, climate event. Even with the toggle, distribution is Gaussian — real failures are bimodal.
  • Life events. Divorce (~40% lifetime probability), job relocation, parent moving in, fertility decisions changing space needs. Any of these can force an early sale (see strip above).
  • Tax law changes. Current-law constants are updated for 2026, but 30 years is enough time for additional structural changes. Treat tax math as illustrative.
  • Behavioral discipline. The model assumes you contribute the discipline-rate × monthly gap consistently. In real drawdowns most people stop contributing — exactly when expected returns are highest.

Framing. "Invest" here means: rent equivalent housing and put every dollar of the difference (down payment, closing costs, plus the monthly gap between owning and renting) into a diversified stock portfolio. You live somewhere either way — the question is where the capital goes.
Methodology. All math is in real (inflation-adjusted) dollars internally. The headline uses the after-exit number on both sides:
  • Buyer side: equity = home value − remaining loan; subtract selling costs (default 6%) and home capital-gains tax (Section 121 exclusion of $250k single / $500k married, with the 2-of-5-year holding rule assumed). Add cumulative tax savings from itemized mortgage interest + SALT deduction (compared against the inflation-indexed standard deduction).
  • Mortgage interest deduction uses the IRC §163(h)(3) post-TCJA $750k principal cap, applied year-by-year via the average outstanding balance method (correct as the loan amortizes below the cap).
  • Investor side: starts with down payment + closing costs in stocks; contributes the monthly differential × your "discipline rate" (Goodman-Mayer 2018: most renters don't invest 100%; 50% is a more honest default). Applies LTCG (0/15/20%) + 3.8% NIIT (IRC §1411) + your state capital-gains rate at exit. Cost basis grows with each contribution.
  • Discount on dividend drag: 0.3%/yr (~1.5% qualified dividend yield × ~18% effective tax rate).
  • Length of stay drives the verdict more than any other variable (Beracha & Johnson 2012). Below ~5 years buying almost always loses.
What this model does NOT do (acknowledged limits):
  • Monte Carlo is optional and still illustrative. Real outcomes vary widely; treat the headline as a scenario result, not a forecast.
  • Single-home idiosyncratic risk is approximate — the toggle adds dispersion, but real home-specific failures are lumpy and local.
  • AMT-lite applies only in the legacy sunset stress mode. Current-law AMT is not tax-prep grade and is intentionally omitted.
  • No emergency-fund / retirement-contribution displacement check (CFP critique). Buying with thin reserves or by cutting 401(k) can destroy the verdict's premise.
  • No metro-conditioned defaults; no rent-risk hedging value (Sinai-Souleles 2005); no leverage / risk-adjusted (CRRA) comparison.
